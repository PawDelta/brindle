Build plan

First the case against building this at all, then the shape I would build, the four choices inside it with their real costs, what each existing guarantee turns into, and the split into tasks. Facts about the backend are from pawdelta-web origin/main at 734a868e and name their file. Anything marked proposal is a number or name I made up to have something to argue with.

B1The design, argued against first

The architecture doc rejects co-editing in 5.3 with three sentences. They deserve an answer one at a time, because one of them is weak and two are real.

  • "A sign-off must name one immutable revision." True, and it is not an argument against live editing. It is an argument for freezing. It only bites if the text people type into and the text people sign have to be the same object. Part 1 makes them two objects, a draft and a version, and the sign-off keeps naming exactly one immutable thing.
  • "The head is one sealed blob rewritten whole on every commit." True, and a real cost. A stream of small changes needs storage the Library does not have (B4.1).
  • "The main author is a supervisor that publishes whole revisions, not keystrokes." True today. It is a design problem (decision D1, B4.5), not a wall.

So 5.3 does not settle it. These six do more damage, and I think any of them is a fair reason to say no.

  1. It needs a live channel, and the hosting is not shaped for one. One FastAPI app serves the marketing site, the account pages and the Library API (backend/main.py), started as a single uvicorn process (Dockerfile). There is no socket route anywhere in backend/. infra/setup.sh creates the service on Fargate at 256 CPU units and 512 MB with a desired count of 1, behind a load balancer and cluster it shares with the stock app; infra/launch_ops.sh raises that to 512 and 1,024 MB, 2 tasks, scaling between 2 and 4 on 60% CPU. Deploys are rolling (200% maximum, 100% minimum healthy) with a 30-second deregistration delay on the target group, so every deploy closes every open socket. No stickiness is configured, so two people in one doc will usually be on different tasks.
  2. It reverses decision 5.1. The system chose "an engine working copy, synced with the Library". A live draft cannot have two authorities: the Library has to hold the draft and the engine becomes one replica of it. Most of the text machinery in pro/shared_docs.py (revision mapping, replaying acceptances in order, recognising a lost answer) is replaced, not extended. That code is built and tested. And the reasons for 5.1 have not gone: Free and Pro plans have no Library, so local revisions stay too, and the engine carries two ways of holding a plan's text for good.
  3. Four guarantees hang on "every write" and cannot stay there. Refusing text that looks like a secret, the drawing-only check on SVG, an audit row, and the read by the Library's model all run once per write today. None can run per letter. Each moves to the freeze or to the session, and each move makes it weaker (B5).
  4. The server sees unfinished thoughts. It is not end to end today either, but today the service sees revisions someone chose to publish. With a live draft it receives every half-sentence and every deleted line, and keeps them until the log is compacted. A customer who was uneasy about section 10.4 should like this less.
  5. There is nobody to co-edit with yet. No client lets a teammate open a shared design (F15; shared_docs._own refuses another account), and the engine has never talked to the real backend (15.2). This would be the third layer on a base no two machines have exercised.
  6. The actual pain has a cheap fix. The pain on record is risk 4: a conflict with no way out. Option A of design/edit-conflict-options.html (their text stands, yours become suggestions) removes it with engine and app work only. If the teams using this are two or three people who seldom edit in the same hour, that plus a faster sync is most of the value for a fraction of the cost. Section locks (5.3 C) do not save anything: showing "Sam is editing" needs the same live channel.

Why I propose it anyway. You asked for it, and three things only a live draft gives. People in the same doc stop losing a round to "the design changed, read it again". The freeze makes "what exactly am I signing" sharper than today's moving head. And the conflict state stops existing instead of being managed. There is also an argument from the current code: every ranged edit today is a full revision that archives the previous one, calls the reader model and counts against a cap of 1,000 (analysis_designs.edit, _revise, config.ANALYSIS_MAX_VERSIONS). An afternoon of two people fixing sentences spends that budget on nothing anyone will sign.

What would change my mind. Task T0 failing (the two merge libraries not agreeing byte for byte). Or you looking at reason 6 and deciding the conflict page's option A is enough for now. Both are cheap to find out before any other task starts.

B2The shape proposed

The draft lives in the Library as an encrypted log of changes plus a snapshot. A small relay holds each open doc in memory, passes changes and carets between the people in it, and appends to the log. Each machine's daemon keeps a replica and one connection per open doc; the app and the supervisor both write through it. Everything about review (comments, votes, sign-offs, reviewers, rules) keeps today's REST calls, plus one new one: freeze.

One relay at a time

The middle column only works if there is never a second relay with its own copy of an open doc. The existing service is created with a maximum of 200% and a minimum healthy of 100% (infra/setup.sh). At a desired count of 1 that means every deploy runs the old and the new task side by side for a while. Two relays would each hold a room for the same doc. The log's order check would stop them taking the same seq, but nothing would carry a change or a caret accepted in one room to the people in the other. Two things prevent it, and the plan needs both.

  • Stop before start. The relay's own service is created with a maximum of 100% and a minimum healthy of 0%, so ECS stops the old task and only then starts the new one. The price is a gap on every relay deploy and every crash. On the stop signal the old relay closes its sockets; people keep typing on their own machines (rendering 6); they reconnect when the new task passes the target group's health check, which with today's settings takes two passes 15 seconds apart after the process is up (infra/setup.sh). The whole gap is not measured; I would hold it to 90 seconds proposal.
  • A fence, because a setting is not a guarantee. A hung task, a slow stop or a relay started by hand can still overlap. One item holds a number that only goes up. A relay raises it when it starts and remembers its own. Every append to any log is a transaction that also checks the number is still that relay's; the task role already has that check. A relay that finds a higher number accepts nothing more, closes every socket and exits. A room where people only move carets makes no appends, so the relay also re-reads the number every 5 seconds proposal while it has anyone connected. The worst case is then two rooms showing each other's carets a few seconds stale, and never two rooms accepting text.
  • Why not a hand-over between old and new. It would remove the gap and needs the cross-task forwarding this design leaves out. At one task and a deploy now and then, a stated gap is cheaper than that machinery. It is the first thing to revisit if the relay ever runs on more than one task.
  • It touches D5. Under D5 B or C a long gap turns someone's typing into suggestions. The line for "long" has to sit above a deploy gap, or every relay deploy does that to everyone at once. My 30 seconds there is too short if the gap is 90.

One detail that follows from the load balancer being shared with the stock app: its settings are not in this repo. The rule that forwards /api/brindle/* exists (infra/setup.sh); whether that balancer keeps an idle socket open long enough, and whether its limit can be changed without touching the other app, can only be checked on AWS (B6).

2 · BYOK revocation and permission loss cut a live session

Today: "Scope keys are not cached, so a revocation takes effect on the next request" (analysis_docs.py). A socket is one request that lasts for hours, so that sentence stops being true by default.

  • A lease. The relay may use a document key for one connection for 60 seconds proposal. To renew, it runs what a read runs: _readable_scope (membership, plan, the share row), _owner_gate, scope_key_bytes (one KMS call), permissions. If any fails, that socket is closed with a reason the app shows (rendering 6), and nothing from it is accepted after its lease ran out. When nobody holds a lease on a key, the key leaves memory.
  • Sooner, for changes we make ourselves. The head gets a counter that every change of access bumps: set_permissions, set_reviewers, share_to, unshare. Each append to the log is a transaction that also checks the counter; the task role already has that check (ConditionCheckItem). A bumped counter fails the next append, and the relay re-checks everyone in the doc at once. Removing someone bites at the next letter typed, not at the end of a lease.
  • An org switching off its own key happens in its AWS account and is invisible until we call KMS. So for BYOK the promise changes from "the next request" to "within one lease". That has to be written where BYOK is described.
  • Cost. One KMS call per connected person per lease: 1,440 a day for someone connected all day at 60 seconds (86,400 ÷ 60). Today's sync costs 2,880 reads a day per plan in review, each with at least one unwrap (architecture §12); the live connection replaces that polling for a doc someone has open.
  • Fix risk 13 first. A design shared from the engine is owned by the author's personal scope under the service key, so an org's key does not cut the author off (architecture 10.4). Until shared designs are created in the team or org scope, nobody should be told a live session obeys BYOK.
  • On the machine. On a cut the daemon deletes its replica and unsent edits for that doc, after offering them to their writer. A modified client can keep what it already had; that is no worse than today.

3 · Secret masking and SVG checks move to the freeze

Today there are three points, all on whole writes: a local write that matches a secret pattern is refused (visuals._refuse_secrets), outgoing bodies are masked (analysis_docs._call), and incoming text is masked before it is stored (architecture N5). A secret typed one letter at a time matches nothing until its last letter, and by then the rest is in the log and on other screens. An SVG is invalid for most of the time someone is typing it.

  1. Before sending, in the daemon. After each local batch the changed section is scanned as it now reads. On a match the batch is held, the app underlines the text and says why, and nothing leaves until it is edited away. A pasted secret, the common case, is caught whole. A secret typed by hand has already sent up to one batch of its letters. That limit is real and should be stated.
  2. At the freeze, on the server. The frozen text is scanned. A match refuses the freeze and names the section. Refuse, not mask: a masked version would not be the text people read.
  3. On arrival, in other daemons. Text is masked before it goes into plans or a supervisor's context, as now. The replica itself holds what was typed.
  4. After removal. A purge: force a compaction so the snapshot no longer carries the deleted letters and the log items that did are gone. T0 has to confirm that deleted text really leaves a compacted state.

SVG. The server's check (design_models._svg) runs at the freeze and refuses. Between freezes each app draws the last source that parses and passes its own filter. DocDiagram.tsx already rebuilds a drawing from an allowlist of elements and drops event handlers, style and class, but its attribute filter is looser than the engine's (plan_docs._SVG_ATTRIBUTES). Between freezes it becomes the only check, so it has to be brought level (task T10). Mermaid has no renderer in the app and is shown as source, so it has nothing to check.

4 · Comment anchors

Today an anchor is a section, two offsets and the quoted words, found again on every read (_locate, _where) and reported as current, moved or detached.

  • In a draft the anchor also carries two positions that travel with the text (Yjs calls them relative positions), made by the app when the comment is written. Text added in front moves them with no searching. If the words between them no longer match the quote, the comment reads "being edited". If the two meet, the words are gone, and the meeting point is "where it was". That is rendering 3.
  • Threads stay server objects written through today's calls, so the server keeps stamping who and when. They are not put in the merged document, because inside it the author of a change is whatever the client says.
  • At a freeze each open thread's positions are resolved against the frozen text and stored in today's form. A version's threads, the REST API and the engine's _where then work unchanged.
  • Hearing about a new comment. The relay sends "threads changed" on the socket and each client fetches them. No comment text travels on the socket.

5 · The supervisor as a participant

  • No connection of its own. It calls the engine over MCP as today. The engine applies its writes to the daemon's replica, and they travel on the author's connection. The server records them under the author's account with an "agent" mark the client sets, which, like from_supervisor today, is the client's word.
  • It answers a text it read a while ago. People keep typing while the supervisor writes. So each time the engine hands it the draft, the engine keeps the exact state it handed over (the replica's state at that instant and the text it produced), under an id the supervisor's write must name proposal: base. A write with no base, or a base the engine no longer holds, is refused and the supervisor reads again.
  • Its changes are worked out against that base, never against the draft as it is now. Comparing its finished revision with the current draft would read "a person added a sentence meanwhile" as "the supervisor deleted that sentence", however small the diff is made. Comparing what it returned with the text it was given, section by section, yields only what the supervisor itself did: these insertions, these deletions, these replacements.
  • Each change is placed by positions that travel. The two ends of every change are fixed in the base state as travelling positions (the same kind B4.4 uses for comments) and resolved in the draft as it stands when the change is applied. An insertion lands beside the same words wherever they have moved; a sentence someone added in the meantime is simply not part of any change.
  • Overlap has one rule. If, since the base, anyone else inserted or deleted anything between the two ends of a replacement or deletion, or deleted the words an insertion was placed beside, that one change is not applied. It is filed as a suggestion showing the supervisor's wording against what stands there now, whatever D1 says, and the rest of the revision still goes in. Two insertions at exactly the same point are both kept, the person's first. Removing or reordering a section follows the same rule by section id: a section changed since the base is not removed, and the removal is filed as a suggestion.
  • A tool for one ranged edit proposal: edit_plan_section, taking what plan_docs.edit takes plus the base, saves it resending the doc. propose_plan on a shared draft goes through the same path.
  • D1 says where the changes go: into the draft (A), into suggestions through plan_docs.suggest as today (B), or held until the section is free and applied as one marked batch (C).
  • It still cannot freeze, sign or approve. approve_plan stays refused and no tool freezes.
  • It writes only while the author's machine is on.
  • Risk 7 grows. A teammate's words already reach the supervisor through comments. Now the draft it reads holds teammates' sentences too. Keep the rule that it reads the draft only when the person sends it (Request changes), and fence teammates' passages as quoted material in that message.

6 · The gate stays on frozen versions

  • plans._gate counts sign-offs that name the Library's head revision when that is the revision on screen. "Revision" becomes "version" and nothing in its rules changes. The server's sign_off still refuses any number but the head's (_on_screen). A draft has no number, so it cannot be signed, by construction.
  • The engine keeps two things apart. plans and plan_revisions hold frozen versions only, imported the way a teammate's revision is today (shared_docs._import). A new plan_drafts holds the replica and the outbox. plans.decide, tasks.build_gate_refusal and plans.holding read only the first, so a build's workers and files always come from a version.
  • One new line in the gate's reason, depending on D3: "a draft has changes since v4" (for information under B), or a withdrawn version reading as waiting (under A and C).
  • Risk 14 is unchanged: an approval is still made on the last review state fetched. The socket can tell the engine sooner that a new version exists, but the conditional call on the server is still the fix.

7 · The conflict state is removed

  • Why it cannot arise. Conflict is gate rule 3: the plan changed here and the Library's head moved too. In this shape the engine never makes a revision of a shared plan by itself. An edit to a shared plan is a change to the draft, and a version is made only by the server, at a freeze, compare-and-set on the head. Two freezes at once: one wins and the other is told v4 exists, with the 409 stale that exists today.
  • What is deleted. In pro/shared_docs.py: _send_text, _send_revisions, _pending_accepts, _landed, and the text half of the local_rev and remote_rev pairing. In plans._gate: rule 3.
  • What stays. _import, now the only way text reaches plans, and the sync of threads, votes and review state.
  • Accepting a suggestion becomes one server call that writes the replacement into the log at the anchor's present position and marks the thread accepted, in one transaction. It no longer makes a version.
  • Plans already in conflict at upgrade. A one-time step: the Library's head is the base, and what changed here is filed as suggestions. That is option A of the conflict page, and it needs your pick from that page first.
  • Plans that are not shared keep local revisions and the 409 on a second window. Two text paths remain in the engine.
  • The first state of a draft is made once, by the server, from the head's text. If two clients each made it, the text would appear twice.

8 · The teammate reader (queued as feat/teammate-design-reader)

Co-editing depends on it and should shape it before it starts. No branch of that name exists in this checkout yet.

  • A teammate's engine has to hold a design it does not own, keyed by the document, not by a session's plan. Today shared_docs._own refuses.
  • First it reads versions and threads over REST and signs off. With the review queue poller that is useful alone.
  • It should never write text as revisions: no call to POST …/edits or …/revisions. A teammate's text will go to the draft. Until the draft exists, the reader reads, comments, suggests and signs.
  • The doc screen should take its content through one interface with two sources (the author's plan, a shared design), so the live draft is one more source behind it.

If the reader ships writing revisions, task T7 has to take that back out.

9 · Audit volume

Today there is one audit row per read and per write (analysis_designs.get, _commit), kept for a year (config.AUDIT_RETENTION), append-only because the task role may not update or delete in that table. One row per change is not possible: at 1-second batches a person typing for an hour makes up to 3,600.

  • Rows per session and per outcome, ids only, as now proposal: joined, left (with counts: batches, bytes, sections touched), cut (with the reason), frozen (the version, the span of the log it covers, who wrote in it), compacted, purged. Sign-offs and the rest keep their rows.
  • Who typed what has to outlive compaction. The freeze panel, the Authors line, "deleted by jonas@ at 14:16" and every option of D4 depend on it, and the log items that carry who and when are exactly what compaction deletes. So the record is kept as follows, and none of it trusts the client.
    1. A replica number belongs to an account's copy of a doc, not to a connection. The merge format stamps every inserted letter with the number of the replica that typed it, and edits typed while disconnected already carry that number when they are replayed. So the number has to last as long as the copy does. The first time a daemon makes its replica of a doc it asks the relay for a number. The relay picks one no one has used in that doc and records "this number, this account, this doc, person or supervisor" as an item in the log before it answers, so the ownership is as durable as the text and reaches the ledger at the next compaction. The daemon stores the number with the replica in SQLite. A second number is registered the same way for that account's supervisor.
    2. Reconnecting presents the number; it does not get a new one. On every connection the daemon names the numbers its replica writes under. The relay accepts each only if the record says it belongs to the signed-in account for this doc, and from then on accepts batches whose letters carry those numbers and no others. That is what lets the outbox replay after a dropped connection, a closed laptop or a relay deploy (rendering 6, B3 A): the relay may have restarted and forgotten every socket, and the ownership is still in the store. A number owned by another account is refused at the claim, and a batch carrying it is refused whoever sends it.
    3. One live writer per number. If a connection claims a number that another live connection holds, the newer one takes over and the older is closed: after a network drop the old socket often lingers. A copy that is behind its own history is a different case (a restored backup, a cloned machine): the relay already holds letters under that number which the claimant does not. Its claim is refused. That daemon registers a fresh number, takes the relay's state, and re-derives its unsent text as new changes against the state it last shared, by the base rule of B4.5.
    4. A number is never reused. After a cut the daemon deletes its replica; the number stays in the record because its letters stay in the draft. If the person's access returns, their new replica gets a new number under the same account.
    5. A ledger is sealed into the snapshot. It holds every registered replica number with its account and mark, and lists what the letters themselves cannot say: who deleted text in which section and when, and for each comment whose words were removed, who removed them and when. The relay works those out as it applies each batch. The ledger is part of the same item as the snapshot (B4.1), so a compaction carries it forward in the one write that replaces the text.
    6. After a restart the relay has the ledger from the snapshot and the clear fields of the items after it. Together that is the whole record since the last version.
  • At a freeze the server walks the frozen text. Each letter's replica number, through the ledger, gives its writer; the state recorded with the previous version says which letters are new since then. The result (per person: the passages and sections they wrote, the sections they deleted in) is sealed into the version, and their ids go into the audit row. The ledger then starts again from that version.
  • If pycrdt cannot show a letter's replica number or check the numbers inside a batch (T0 asks), the fallback is heavier and gives the same record: on each append the relay notes the ranges that batch inserted, as travelling positions, in the ledger.
  • Net. For a doc someone has open, joined and left replace the 2,880 polled reads a day. Not measured.
  • Open. If an Enterprise customer needs every change kept, compaction has to archive instead of delete, and storage grows without bound per doc.

B6What can only be tested on a real server

The engine has only ever run against a hand-written fake of the Library (architecture risk 1). This adds a second protocol to that gap. None of the following can be proved in either repo's tests.

  • A WebSocket through the shared load balancer. That the upgrade passes the /api/brindle/* rule; how long that balancer leaves an idle socket open (its settings belong to the stock app's setup, not this repo); how often the relay must ping to stay under it.
  • A relay deploy with people typing. That ECS really stops the old task before it starts the new one under the relay's own settings, with no moment when both pass the health check; how long the gap is from the last accepted change to the first one after; that every client reconnects by itself and nothing typed during the gap is lost or doubled.
  • Two relays on purpose. Start a second relay by hand beside the first. The first must refuse its next append, close its sockets within the fence's interval and exit, and no change may be accepted by both.
  • DynamoDB itself. A conditional append under two writers, a transaction with the access check, a 300 KiB item, and above all the time one append takes, because that is the delay between one person's letter and the other's screen.
  • KMS at lease pace. Call volume and throttling with real numbers of people connected.
  • BYOK from a second AWS account. Switch the key off there and watch every session close within one lease, the author's included (after risk 13 is fixed).
  • Memory. How many open docs a 512-unit, 1,024 MB Fargate task holds, and what a much-edited design's snapshot weighs.
  • Two machines, two accounts. Real clocks that disagree, a laptop closed for an hour and reopened, the app quit with unsent edits.
  • The app's window on each OS. Typing with an input method (accents, Japanese) while someone else's letters arrive in the same line, in the built app. A staff build on a real machine, not a server, but not a unit test either.
